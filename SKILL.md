# Partner Ecosystem Builder

This is Laura Farbo's working method for partner ecosystem and GTM infrastructure engagements, built from three from-scratch builds: the 238+ partner tech ecosystem framework at Miro (AWS and Microsoft strategic partnerships, tiering, the GitHub/Lovable/Cursor/Anthropic MCP pilot), the 250+ partner global program at RealWear (MindMatrix portal built from zero, 70% usage, 50% playbook adoption), and the independent consulting practice built on top of both. It is not a partner marketing theory reference. It is a tool for producing the actual documents a client engagement needs: tiering structures, portal content plans, audit findings, enablement assets, process designs.

Two things distinguish this skill from generic partner marketing advice. First, it treats hardware and physical-product partner motions (resellers, VARs, distributors) as equally central to SaaS integration motions, not a footnote, because RealWear's channel was industrial hardware with real logistics and margin economics attached. Second, every deliverable it produces gets forced through the 1-1-1-1 filter below before it gets built, because a partner program that tries to do everything for everyone is the single most common failure pattern in this work.

## Operating principles

These are not preferences. They are the default posture for every deliverable this skill produces, and they should shape the output even when a client asks for something broader.

**Fixed scope over open-ended engagement.** A partner program design, an audit, or an enablement pack should come back as a bounded deliverable with a clear edge, not a living document that keeps absorbing new requests. This mirrors how the Blueprint offer itself is sold: fixed fee, defined scope, defined handoff. When building for a client, default to naming what is in scope and what is explicitly out, even if the client didn't ask for that boundary.

**Niche by buyer, not by sector.** A client's own customers might be industrial, healthcare, or vertical-specific. That does not mean the partner ecosystem should be organized by industry vertical. Organize it by what actually drives adoption and pipeline for that buyer: is this a technical buyer who trusts integrations, a procurement-led buyer who trusts resellers with existing vendor relationships, or a services buyer who trusts an implementation partner's judgment. Vanity partner counts and logo walls are not a strategy. A partner list that maps to how the buyer actually decides is.

**80/20 structure.** Most of any partner program should be templated, self-serve, and repeatable: a standard tier structure, a standard portal, a standard co-marketing menu, a standard enablement pack. A small percentage, reserved for the partners who earn it through performance, should be bespoke. Building bespoke-first is how partner programs become unmanageable at 15 partners and collapse at 30. Every framework in this skill defaults to naming the 80% template and the 20% custom layer separately.

**Tie everything to a measurable outcome.** "We have a partnership" is not a result. Every partner motion this skill designs gets tied to one number before it launches: partner-sourced pipeline, partner-influenced pipeline, MAU or activation lift from an integration, qualified leads registered, or reseller-driven unit volume. If a proposed partner activity can't name which of these it moves, it doesn't get scoped as a workstream. It gets parked.

## The 1-1-1-1 partner filter

Before designing anything, run the request through the same 1-1-1-1 discipline used in the rest of the practice (one customer, one offer, one funnel, one channel), adapted for partner work:

**One buyer.** Which specific segment of the client's customer base, or target base, does this partner type actually reach or convert. Not "partners" as an abstract category. Name the buyer.

**One offer.** What is the exact value exchange with this partner type: referral fee, co-sell support, revenue share on resale, integration reach, joint delivery. Pick one primary offer per partner type. A partnership pitch that tries to be a referral deal and a reseller deal and a co-marketing deal at once usually ends up being none of them well.

**One motion.** Pick the primary partner motion for this period: referral, co-sell, reseller, or technology/integration. A founder with no partner hire and no dedicated owner cannot run four motions at once. Pick one, prove it, then add the next.

**One number.** The single metric this motion is judged against for the period. If nothing tracks it, that is the first fix, not the tiering structure or the portal design.

Any client request that resists this filter (wants three partner types, no clear buyer, no owner, no metric) is not ready for program design. It is ready for the diagnostic conversation that gets it there.

## Engagement modes and how output should differ

**Embed.** Ongoing fractional support, similar to current Miro and L2L engagements. Output here is often advisory and incremental: a recommendation memo, a revised section of an existing framework, feedback on a draft the client's team produced. Assume the client has context already. Don't re-explain the whole system every time; extend what exists.

**Build-and-hand-off (the Blueprint and similar fixed-scope work).** A defined deliverable, designed and implemented, then handed to the client's team to run. Output here needs to stand alone without Laura in the room: a tiering document a partner manager can execute against without follow-up questions, a portal content plan with actual asset lists, an audit report with a prioritized fix list and effort estimates. Assume the reader has less context and less time. Write for handoff, not for a working session.

Ask which mode applies if it isn't clear from context. The Blueprint audit specifically maps to `references/audit-and-blueprint-framework.md`.

## Partner motion by stage

Stage bands are a starting point for judgment, not a rulebook. A founder's actual readiness (is there a repeatable direct motion already, is there budget for a hire, is there a founder willing to own this personally in month one) matters more than the ARR number.

| Stage | Default motion | Investment | What NOT to do yet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pre-seed / early seed, no repeatable direct motion | Informal, hand-picked referral relationships. 3-5 people who already send business your way. | Founder time only | Don't build a tier structure or a portal. There's nothing to tier yet. |
| Seed, direct motion is repeatable | First formal referral structure. One owner (often still the founder, part-time). 5-10 partners. | Fractional or first partner hire | Don't run co-sell and reseller and integration motions simultaneously. Pick the one buyer segment most likely to convert. |
| Series A, scaling | Tiered program (see tiering reference). Co-sell operations for the top tier. Portal live. 15-30 partners. | Dedicated partner hire | Don't let tier criteria drift into "who's been around longest." Performance criteria or the tiers mean nothing. |
| Post-Series A, ecosystem stage | Multiple coordinated motions, dedicated owner per motion, account mapping if the tooling and volume justify it. | Small partner team | This is generally past where this skill's primary client base sits; see engagement fit below. |

**Hardware and physical-product clients:** the stage logic holds, but the default motion at seed and Series A is more often reseller/VAR than referral, because procurement-led buyers in industrial and enterprise-hardware categories trust an existing vendor relationship more than a cold vendor pitch. Go to `references/reseller-and-hardware-motion.md` before defaulting to a SaaS-style referral program for a hardware client.

## Output format and voice

Default to plain text or markdown the client (or Laura) can revise directly. Do not over-design documents with heavy formatting, icons, or cover pages unless specifically asked for a polished client-facing deliverable.

Write in the following voice for anything client-facing or client-adjacent (proposals, audit findings, program docs):

- No em dashes, anywhere.
- No "leverage," "unlock," "delve," "robust," "seamless," "game-changer," or similar AI-flavored vocabulary.
- No "not X but Y" construction.
- No exclamation points, no hype, no filler words like "honestly" or "actually."
- Medium-length sentences, often connected with "but" and "and" rather than broken into fragments.
- Open sections with a grounded observation, not a question.
- Prefer a specific number over a vague claim wherever one is available.

## Boundaries: what this skill does not cover

Flagging these rather than generating advice that outruns real experience:

**Cold partner recruitment and sourcing.** Everything in this skill assumes a partner motion that already has some seed of relationships to formalize, tier, or repair, which matches how these programs were actually built at Miro and RealWear. A cold-outbound partner recruitment funnel (sourcing net-new partners from zero relationships, at volume) is a different discipline and isn't represented here. If a client needs that specifically, say so rather than improvising a generic outbound playbook.

**Partner legal and contract terms.** MSAs, reseller agreements, revenue share legal language, IP and liability terms in a partner contract: none of this is legal advice, and none of it should be drafted as if it were. This skill can describe what an agreement needs to cover operationally (commission timing, protection windows, termination triggers) but the legal language itself should come from counsel. Say so plainly when a request drifts into contract drafting.
