# Co-Marketing Menus and Launch Playbooks

## The co-marketing menu (campaign-in-a-box)

The point of a co-marketing menu is that most partners should be able to run a joint campaign without a custom brief every time. Build the menu once, well, and let partners pick from it. This is the 80% of the 80/20 structure; the bespoke layer is covered at the end of this section.

### Menu structure

Offer a short list of packaged options, each fully templated, each with a defined partner effort level attached so a partner can pick based on what they can actually commit to:

**Co-branded one-pager.** Lowest effort. Template with the client's positioning on one side, space for the partner's logo and a short custom paragraph on the other. Partner effort: review and approve, maybe supply a quote. Turnaround: days, not weeks.

**Joint webinar.** Templated slide deck structure, templated promotional email and social copy, templated registration page. Partner effort: co-present, promote to their list. Turnaround: 3-4 weeks from scheduling to delivery, mostly driven by calendar coordination, not content creation.

**Social kit.** Pre-written, pre-designed social posts for both sides to publish, timed together. Partner effort: schedule and publish. Turnaround: days.

**Email sequence.** A short templated sequence (2-3 emails) either side can send to their own list, introducing the joint value proposition. Partner effort: review, approve sender details, hit send. Turnaround: days to a week.

**Case study / joint customer story.** Requires an actual shared customer willing to participate. Templated interview questions and write-up structure, but the content itself can't be fully pre-built since it depends on a real story. Partner effort: identify the customer, help secure participation. Turnaround: 4-6 weeks, mostly dependent on customer scheduling.

### Choosing what a given partner gets

Explorer tier: access to the one-pager and social kit only, self-serve, no partner-team hours required beyond providing the templates.

Builder tier: full menu access, including the webinar and email sequence, with light partner-team coordination (scheduling, review).

Innovator tier: the menu plus the bespoke layer below.

### The bespoke 20% layer (Innovator tier)

Reserve custom campaign work for partners who've earned it through tier performance, not for whoever asks loudest. Bespoke work might include a jointly branded microsite, a custom research report co-produced with the partner, or a multi-touch campaign built around a specific shared account list. Scope this the same way any fixed-scope engagement gets scoped: a defined deliverable, a defined timeline, and a defined end, not an open-ended co-marketing relationship that never resolves.

## Launch playbook: product or integration launch with a partner

This structure comes out of running a technology/integration partner launch (the MCP pilot with GitHub, Lovable, Cursor, and Anthropic), where the partner relationship is technical as well as commercial, and the launch has to work for both a product audience and a partner audience at once.

### Phase 1: Pre-launch alignment (4-6 weeks out)

- Confirm the actual scope of what's launching: is this a full integration, a pilot with a defined partner list, or a broader marketplace-style launch. Scope drift here is the most common cause of a launch date slipping.
- Align on what each side is responsible for publishing (blog post, changelog entry, documentation) and who reviews what before it goes out. Technical partners in particular will want to review any technical claim about how the integration works.
- Confirm the metric this launch is judged against before launch day, not after (see the one-number rule in the main skill file): activation of the integration, MAU lift, qualified leads from the partner's audience, or something else specific to this launch.

### Phase 2: Technical and enablement readiness (2-4 weeks out)

- Confirm the integration or feature is actually stable enough to demo and support at launch. A launch date should never outrun technical readiness; slipping the date is cheaper than launching something that breaks in front of the partner's audience.
- Build the enablement partners need to talk about the launch accurately: a short internal brief for the partner's own team (what it is, why it matters, common questions), not just external marketing copy.
- If this is a pilot with a small, named partner list (as the MCP pilot was), confirm each named partner has what they need to participate before the public announcement, so the announcement isn't the first time they're seeing the final version.

### Phase 3: Go-live

- Coordinate publish timing across both sides. A joint launch where one side posts a day early undercuts the "joint" framing.
- Have a single point of contact on each side for launch-day issues (a broken link, a technical question that surfaces publicly, a press or community reaction that needs a fast response).

### Phase 4: Post-launch measurement (2-4 weeks after)

- Report against the metric defined in Phase 1, not a substitute metric that happens to look better.
- If the launch was a pilot, use this window to decide explicitly whether it expands (more partners, broader availability) or stays limited, rather than letting it drift into permanent-pilot status with no decision made.
- Feed whatever was learned about partner-side friction (unclear docs, slow technical support, confusing enablement) back into the enablement materials before the next launch, not after the next one also struggles with the same issue.
